\section{EXTRAPOLATIONS TO THE PHYSICAL LIMIT}
\label{sec:physical_limit_extrapolation}

Combining the results obtained at each momentum in Section~\ref{sec:simultaneous_fit_strategy}, we extrapolate them to $\hat{\zeta}\to\infty$, which for the Sivers shift is the physical limit. Section~\ref{sec:zetahat_extrapolation_motivation} shows that the amplitudes, the TMD combinations, and the Sivers shift change systematically with $1/\hat\zeta$ across the four simulated momenta. Section~\ref{sec:extrap_fit_param_level} extrapolates the fit parameters to $1/\hat\zeta\to0$ using $P_L=-2,-3,-4$, reconstructs the amplitudes and the Sivers shift in this limit, and lists the numerical Fourier-transform integrals over $x$ at $|b_T|=3a$. Section~\ref{sec:factorization_xbt} tests factorization, first in $(P\cdot b)$ and $b^2$ on the lattice data at finite momentum, and then in $x$ and $|b_T|$ for the extrapolated Sivers shift and amplitudes.

\subsection{Motivation for the \texorpdfstring{$\hat{\zeta} \to \infty$}{TEXT} Extrapolation}
\label{sec:zetahat_extrapolation_motivation}

Chapter~\ref{sec:fitting_methodologies} established stable global simultaneous fits to the invariant amplitudes $\tAmp_{2B}$ and $\tAmp_{12B}$ at each of our four simulated nucleon momenta, corresponding to the finite Collins-Soper parameters $\hat\zeta = 0.090772$, $0.294367$, $0.539684$, $0.785756$ for $P_L=-1,-2,-3,-4$ respectively (Table~\ref{tab:zeta_directions}). As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:collins_soper_parameter}, the fully quantitative predictions for, or a comparison with, 
phenomenological and experimental TMD studies employing QCD factorization
would require lattice data for much larger Collins-Soper parameters,
$\hat\zeta \gg 1$.
With $\hat \zeta^2=- (v \tcdot P)^2/(v^2\mN^2)$, the limit $\hat\zeta\rightarrow\infty$ 
corresponds to the limit of a light-like staple direction $v$,
or an infinite rapidity $y_v\rightarrow -\infty$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq-zeta}). 

Figures~\ref{fig:zetahat_discrete_slices_amps}--\ref{fig:zetahat_discrete_slices_sivers} show why we need this extrapolation. At fixed transverse quark separation $|b_T| = 3a \approx 0.34$ fm, we evaluate the reconstructed amplitudes $\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{2B}$ and $\tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B}$, the corresponding unpolarized and Sivers TMD combinations $\tilde f_1^{(0)} = 2\tAmp^{[x]}_{2B}$ and $\tilde f_{1T}^{\perp(1)} = -2\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{12B}$, and the resulting Sivers shift $\langle k_y \rangle_{TU}$ and $x\langle k_y \rangle_{TU}$, as continuous functions of $x$ at each of our four simulated momenta. Plotted against $1/\hat\zeta$, all six quantities vary systematically with the momentum. 

For the Sivers shift defined in Eq.~(\ref{eq-genSivShift}), 
where the soft factors in the TMD definitions \cite{Ji:2004wu,Aybat:2011zv,CollinsBook2011}
cancel out, large values of $\hat\zeta$ can also be accessed through large nucleon momenta.
Clearly, the limit of an infinite Collins-Soper parameter is in practice not accessible on the lattice, so that
we must rely on results for a limited range of $\hat \zeta$, as in Figures~\ref{fig:zetahat_discrete_slices_amps}--\ref{fig:zetahat_discrete_slices_sivers}.
From the perturbative prediction for the $\zeta$ dependence, cf., e.g., Ref.~\cite{Aybat:2011zv}, we would expect that 
the ratios of TMDs should become independent of $\hat\zeta$ as $\hat\zeta\rightarrow\infty$.

\subsection{Extrapolation \texorpdfstring{$\hat{\zeta} \to \infty$}{TEXT}}
\label{sec:extrap_fit_param_level}

The lowest simulated momentum, $P_L=-1$ ($\hat\zeta = 0.090772$), lies at $1/\hat\zeta = 11.02$, far from the other three momenta ($1/\hat\zeta = 3.40$, $1.85$, $1.27$) and from the asymptotic region $1/\hat\zeta \to 0$ that the extrapolation targets. A single point that far out would dominate the two-parameter fits introduced below and bias the extrapolated intercept rather than constrain it. We therefore exclude $P_L=-1$ and extrapolate using only the three higher momenta $P_L = -2,-3,-4$.

Rather than extrapolate the amplitudes point by point in $x$, we extrapolate each of the twelve parameters of the global simultaneous-fit models directly: the four parameters $\{a,c,d,j\}$ appearing in each of the power-law models for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$ (Eq.~(\ref{eq:model_ReA2B})-(\ref{eq:model_ImA2B})).
% TODO(Hari): the global fit also has f, k1, k2 (Fit_Table_PL-1). State how they enter the extrapolation (f cancels in the Sivers shift, but what about the amplitude plots, and how is c(eta) with k1, k2 evaluated?).
In these models $c$ multiplies $b_L^2 = (P\cdot b)^2/P_L^2$ and $a_{\text{im}}$ multiplies $b_L = (P\cdot b)/(-P_L)$, so the parameters $c_{\text{re}}$, $c_{\text{im}}$, and $c_{\text{reA12B}}$ are first rescaled by $1/P_L^2$ and $a_{\text{im}}$ by $1/P_L$. After rescaling, they multiply powers of the invariant $P\cdot b$ directly (up to an overall sign for $a_{\text{im}}$), so all three momenta can be fit on a common footing. For the resulting $\hat\zeta$-dependence of each rescaled parameter, we tested two functional forms (as explored in \cite{Engelhardt:2015xja}),
\begin{equation}
    p(\hat\zeta) = c + \frac{d}{\hat\zeta} \qquad \text{and} \qquad p(\hat\zeta) = c + \frac{d}{\hat\zeta^2} ,
    \label{eq:zetahat_extrap_ansatz}
\end{equation}
using correlated Jackknife fits to the three data points. The quadratic ansatz consistently gave a lower $\chi^2/\text{dof}$ than the linear form across the twelve parameters, and we adopt it for every extrapolation shown below. The extrapolated value of each parameter at $\hat\zeta \to \infty$ is its fitted intercept $c$ at $1/\hat\zeta^2=0$.

Restricting the integration to $x\in\{-1,0\}$ or $\{0,1\}$ isolates the antiquark and quark contributions respectively; because $\tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B}$ is an odd function of $x$ while $\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{2B}$ and $\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{12B}$ are even, the antiquark and quark ranges give equal $\int dx\,\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{2B}$ and $\int dx\,\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{12B}$ but opposite-sign $\int dx\,(i\tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B})$, so the combined $\{-1,1\}$ integral of the imaginary term is consistent with zero within uncertainty. The integrals over the full range $\{-\infty,\infty\}$ agree with those over the physically motivated range $\{-1,1\}$ well within uncertainties. The extrapolated amplitudes therefore decay fast enough in $x$ that truncating at the light-cone-momentum-fraction cutoff is negligible.

\subsection{Testing Factorization in \texorpdfstring{$x$}{TEXT} and \texorpdfstring{$|b_T|$}{TEXT} at \texorpdfstring{$\hat{\zeta} \to \infty$}{TEXT}}
\label{sec:factorization_xbt}

We test factorization at two levels. First, we check whether the lattice amplitudes factorize in $(P\cdot b)$ and $b^2$ at finite momentum. We then ask whether the extrapolated distributions factorize in the momentum fraction $x$ and the transverse separation $|b_T|$.

The selected amplitude model and the other ans\"atze of Chapter~\ref{sec:fitting_methodologies} are not products of separate functions of $(P\cdot b)$ and $b^2$. The Gaussian model of Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_transformation}, which is such a product, fails; the \texttt{PySR} search of Section~\ref{sec:pysr_methodology} found no such product; and the separable models of Sections~\ref{sec:systematic_exploration} and~\ref{sec:rejection_exponential_longitudinal} fail as well. Section~\ref{sec:ratio_test} checks the question directly on the lattice data at finite momentum.

\subsubsection{Ratio Test in \texorpdfstring{$(P\cdot b, b^2)$}{TEXT} on the Lattice Data}
\label{sec:ratio_test}

We first check directly on the lattice data whether an amplitude can be written as a product of longitudinal and transverse profiles, $\tAmp(P\cdot b,b^2) = F(P\cdot b)\,G(b^2)$. If it can, any ratio taken at a common $b^2$ between two values of $P\cdot b$ is a constant in $b^2$, and any ratio taken at a common $P\cdot b$ between two values of $b^2$ does not depend on $P\cdot b$. We form
\begin{align}
    T(P\cdot b, b^2) &= \frac{\tAmp(P\cdot b, b^2)}{\tAmp(P\cdot b = 4, b^2)} \overset{?}{=} \frac{F(P\cdot b)}{F(4)} \ , \label{eq:factorization_T}\\
    B(P\cdot b, b^2) &= \frac{\tAmp(P\cdot b, b^2)}{\tAmp(P\cdot b, b^2 = 18)} \overset{?}{=} \frac{G(b^2)}{G(18)} \ , \label{eq:factorization_B}
\end{align}
where the second equality in each line holds only under factorization. $T$ should be flat in $b^2$ and $B$ should be flat in $P\cdot b$. Both ratios are computed on each Jackknife sample, and the error comes from the Jackknife spread of the ratio.

The limitation of this test lies in how the data are sampled. At fixed $\eta$ and $P_L$, the $(b_L, b_T)$ points computed on the lattice map onto an irregular set of $(P\cdot b, b^2)$ pairs, listed in Table~\ref{tab:Pb_bsq_pairs} for $\eta|v|/a = 8$ and $P_L = -1$. There are 34 pairs, but the rows at $\pm P\cdot b$ are symmetric copies of each other (identical for the real amplitudes and opposite in sign for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$), so only $P\cdot b \ge 0$ carries independent information. At each $(P\cdot b, b^2)$, the two points with $\pm b_T$ are independent measurements, and we average them sample by sample. After the cut $|\vect{b}| \ge 3a$, that is $b^2 \ge 9$, only three values of $P\cdot b$ have more than one $b^2$: $P\cdot b = 0$ with $b^2 = 9, 16, 25, 36, 49$; $P\cdot b = 3$ with $b^2 = 18, 45$; and $P\cdot b = 4$ with $b^2 = 17, 20, 32$. No two of these rows share two $b^2$ values, so the ratios in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:factorization_T} and~\eqref{eq:factorization_B} cannot be built from data alone.

On the $P\cdot b = 0$ and $P\cdot b = 4$ rows, we use quadratic interpolation in $b^2$: the value at a grid point comes from the parabola through the three data points on the same row that lie closest to it. The $P\cdot b = 3$ row has only two data points, $b^2 = 18$ and $45$, so there we use linear interpolation between them. There is no extrapolation. As a check, Figure~\ref{fig:Factorization_ratios_Pb034_eta8_PL-1} also shows the ratios obtained with linear interpolation on all three rows, between the nearest data points above and below each grid value. Table~\ref{tab:factorization_grid} lists which grid values are data and which are interpolated: 4 of the 12 are data, and the other 8 are interpolated. For $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, which is odd in $P\cdot b$ and vanishes at $P\cdot b = 0$, only the $P\cdot b = 3$ and $4$ rows are used.

From Figure~\ref{fig:Factorization_ratios_Pb034_eta8_PL-1}, in certain region $P\cdot b=0$, the ratio $T(P\cdot b, b^2) = \frac{\tAmp(P\cdot b, b^2)}{\tAmp(P\cdot b = 4, b^2)}$ is flat but for $P\cdot b=3$, the ratio $T$ is increasing monotonically. The same behavior is captured in both linear and quadratic interpolation. The other ratio $B(P\cdot b, b^2) = \frac{\tAmp(P\cdot b, b^2)}{\tAmp(P\cdot b, b^2 = 18)}$ is not flat, and there exists a cut in continuity at $P\cdot b=3$ which could also arise from systematic uncertainty from interpolation and limitation of data. 

The ratio test is thus only a qualitative check. Where the interpolated values rest on closely spaced data, the $P\cdot b = 0$ and $4$ rows are compatible with factorization, but the sampling is too sparse to settle the question. The separable fits of Section~\ref{sec:rejection_exponential_longitudinal} lead to the same conclusion: no separable form describes all three amplitudes.

\subsubsection{Factorization in \texorpdfstring{$x$}{TEXT} and \texorpdfstring{$|b_T|$}{TEXT} at \texorpdfstring{$\hat{\zeta} \to \infty$}{TEXT}}
\label{sec:factorization_xbt_form}


Figures~\ref{fig:extrapolated_amplitude_3d_amps} and~\ref{fig:extrapolated_amplitude_3d_sivers} show the joint dependence of the extrapolated amplitudes and Sivers shift on both $x$ and $|b_T|$. We ask whether this joint dependence is separable, that is, whether the extrapolated Sivers shift factorizes as
\begin{equation}
    \langle k_y \rangle_{TU}(x,|b_T|) \overset{?}{=} F(x)\,G(|b_T|) \ ,
\end{equation}
with all of the $x$-shape carried by $F(x)$ and all of the $|b_T|$-shape by $G(|b_T|)$. Such a factorization is not automatic in our fit ansatz for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$ (Eq.~(\ref{eq:model_ReA2B})-(\ref{eq:model_ImA2B})). After Fourier transformation, each amplitude enters only through the combination $d_X(b^2) = 1 + d_X\,b^2$ inside a Bessel function that also carries the $x$-dependence,
\begin{equation}
    \tAmp^{[x]}_X(x,b^2) \ \propto\ N_X(b^2)\ |x|^{-1/2+j_X}\ K_{\nu_X}\!\left(|x|\sqrt{d_X(b^2)/c_X}\right) \ ,
\end{equation}
% TODO(Hari): this closed form (and nu_X, N_X) is not derived earlier in the thesis. Add the Fourier transform of the power-law model in the Fitting chapter (Simultaneous Fitting Strategy) or an appendix, and cite it here.
so $|b_T|$ rescales the argument of the same Bessel function that carries the $x$-dependence, rather than multiplying it by an overall $x$-independent prefactor. The Sivers shift is a ratio of three such amplitudes ($\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{2B}$, $\tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B}$, $\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{12B}$), each with its own independently fitted $(j_X,d_X)$; an exact factorization would require these parameters to coincide across the three amplitudes so that the $x$- and $|b_T|$-dependence separates. Whether the extrapolated fit comes close enough to that coincidence is a numerical question, which we answer below.

Section~\ref{sec:factorization_metric} defines the relative distance from a factorized form, and Sections~\ref{sec:factorization_sivers_test} and~\ref{sec:factorization_amplitude_test} apply it to the Sivers shift and to the individual amplitudes.

\subsubsection{Quantifying the Distance from a Factorized Form}
\label{sec:factorization_metric}

Given a function of two variables $f(x,|b_T|)$, we quantify how far it is from a factorized form $g(x)h(|b_T|)$ by finding the closest such pair in the least-squares sense and reporting the residual. Explicitly, we minimize
\begin{equation}
    J = \int dx\, d|b_T| \, \big(f(x,|b_T|) - g(x)h(|b_T|)\big)^2
\end{equation}
over all functions $g$ and $h$. Setting the functional derivatives with respect to $g(x)$ and $h(|b_T|)$ to zero gives
\begin{align}
    0 &= \frac{\delta J}{\delta g(x)} = \int d|b_T|\, f(x,|b_T|)\,h(|b_T|) - g(x)\int d|b_T|\, h^2(|b_T|) \ , \\
    0 &= \frac{\delta J}{\delta h(|b_T|)} = \int dx\, f(x,|b_T|)\,g(x) - h(|b_T|)\int dx\, g^2(x) \ ,
\end{align}
which can be solved for $h$ in terms of $g$,
\begin{equation}
    h(|b_T|) = \frac{\int dx\, f(x,|b_T|)\,g(x)}{\int dx\, g^2(x)} \ ,
    \label{eq:factorization_h_solution}
\end{equation}
and reinserted to eliminate $h$, giving a closed, self-consistent equation for $g$ alone:
\begin{equation}
    g(x) = \left[\int d|b_T| \left[\frac{\int dx'\, f(x',|b_T|)\,g(x')}{\int dx'\, g^2(x')}\right]^2\right]^{-1} \int d|b_T|\, f(x,|b_T|)\,\frac{\int dx'\, f(x',|b_T|)\,g(x')}{\int dx'\, g^2(x')} \ .
    \label{eq:factorization_g_selfconsistent}
\end{equation}
We solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:factorization_g_selfconsistent} iteratively. Starting from an initial guess $g=1$, we evaluate the right-hand side to obtain a refined $g$, reinsert it, and repeat until $g$ (and the resulting relative distance below) stops changing within a fixed tolerance.

We then compare the residual $\sqrt{J}$ of the optimal separable pair $g(x)h(|b_T|)$ with the overall size of $f$, $N = \int dx\, d|b_T|\, f^2(x,|b_T|)$, which gives the relative distance from a factorized form:
\begin{equation}
    \text{relative distance} = \sqrt{J/N} \ .
    \label{eq:factorization_relative_distance}
\end{equation}
A relative distance of zero indicates exact factorization; the closer to zero, the better $F(x)G(|b_T|)$ approximates the true joint $(x,|b_T|)$ dependence. In practice, we replace the continuum integrals with sums over a discrete $(x,|b_T|)$ grid, apply the iteration of Eq.~\eqref{eq:factorization_g_selfconsistent} independently to each Jackknife sample of the extrapolated amplitudes, and reduce the resulting distribution of relative distances to a Jackknife mean and error. We repeat the full test in four $x$-windows, $[0,1]$, $[0.1,0.9]$, $[0.2,0.8]$, and $[0.3,0.8]$, to check that the conclusion is not an artifact of the amplitudes' behavior near $x=0$.

\subsubsection{Factorization Test for the Sivers Shift}
\label{sec:factorization_sivers_test}

The extrapolated fit parameters already suggest, before any distance test, that exact factorization is unlikely. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:factorization_xbt_form}, factorization requires the Bessel-function order $j_X$ and width parameter $d_X$ to coincide across the three input amplitudes; the extrapolated values are
\begin{equation}
    j_{\text{re}} = 2.0667(69), \quad j_{\text{im}} = 0.9921(92), \quad j_{\text{reA12B}} = 2.0026(40) \ ,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{re}} = 0.139(12), \quad d_{\text{im}} = 0.183(14), \quad d_{\text{reA12B}} = 0.105(18) \ .
\end{equation}
The two $j$ parameters entering the real amplitudes, $j_{\text{re}}$ and $j_{\text{reA12B}}$, agree with each other, but $j_{\text{im}} \approx 1$ differs clearly from both. The three $d$ parameters lie closer together but are still resolved from one another well outside their Jackknife uncertainties. This mismatch in the parameters, especially in $j$, gives a separate reason to expect a nonzero residual from exact factorization.

Ratios of the Sivers shift at fixed $x$ or fixed $|b_T|$ show the same non-factorization more directly. If $\langle k_y \rangle_{TU}(x,|b_T|) = F(x)G(|b_T|)$ exactly, then the ratios
\begin{align}
    R_b(x) &\equiv \frac{\langle k_y \rangle_{TU}(x,|b_T|)}{\langle k_y \rangle_{TU}(x,|b_T|_{\text{ref}})} = \frac{G(|b_T|)}{G(|b_T|_{\text{ref}})} \ , \label{eq:ratio_Rb}\\
    R_x(|b_T|) &\equiv \frac{\langle k_y \rangle_{TU}(x,|b_T|)}{\langle k_y \rangle_{TU}(x_{\text{ref}},|b_T|)} = \frac{F(x)}{F(x_{\text{ref}})} \label{eq:ratio_Rx}
\end{align}
must be flat in $x$ for every $|b_T|$ and reference $|b_T|_{\text{ref}}$, and flat in $|b_T|$ for every $x$ and $x_{\text{ref}}$, respectively. Requiring this for every pair of references is both necessary and sufficient for factorization, so the ratio test is a complete test rather than a heuristic proxy. Unlike the lattice-data test of Section~\ref{sec:ratio_test}, which is limited by the sparse $(P\cdot b, b^2)$ sampling, these ratios use the continuous reconstructed functions, so they can be formed for any pair of references. Figures~\ref{fig:factorization_ratio_x_sivers} and~\ref{fig:factorization_ratio_bT_sivers} show that neither ratio is flat.

The Sivers-shift and amplitude-level tests agree. Neither the individual amplitudes nor the Sivers-shift ratio built from them is exactly factorized in $x$ and $|b_T|$, but the deviation from a factorized form is a modest, well-resolved residual and not a qualitative failure. The Sivers shift itself is factorized to within roughly $5.7$-$7.6\%$ in the relative $L_2$ sense. This is smaller than the $8$-$19\%$ residuals of the amplitudes that enter it, which points to a partial cancellation of non-factorized behavior in the ratio. The residual traces back directly to the extrapolated fit parameters: the Bessel-function order $j_{\text{im}}\approx1$ governing $\tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B}$ differs from $j_{\text{re}},j_{\text{reA12B}}\approx2$ governing the two real amplitudes, and the three width parameters $d_X$ are likewise resolved from one another well outside their Jackknife uncertainties. We conclude that an exactly factorized ansatz $F(x)G(|b_T|)$ would be a good, but not exact, approximation to the extracted Sivers shift.
