\section{FITTING METHODOLOGIES AND STABILITY ANALYSIS}
\label{sec:fitting_methodologies}

As discussed in the previous section (Sec. \ref{sec:x_dependence}), the lattice data show varying statistical precision among the invariant amplitudes. The real part of the unpolarized amplitude, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, has small statistical uncertainties across the simulated position space, whereas the imaginary unpolarized amplitude $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$ and the real Sivers amplitude $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$ have larger gauge noise. We already discarded the imaginary Sivers amplitude $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Im}}$ because it is consistent with zero. We first establish a stable global fit for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ as the baseline for the remaining numerical analysis, as it corresponds to the leading-twist unpolarized TMD and has the highest data quality.

We parameterize the amplitudes in the Cartesian space $(b_L, b_T)$ rather than the Lorentz-invariant variable space $(P \cdot b, b^2)$. Fitting in $(b_L, b_T)$ provides a larger, more uniformly distributed set of discrete data points and resolves kinematic degeneracies. Because the squared separation distance $b^2 = b_L^2 + b_T^2$ is non-negative, multiple distinct combinations of longitudinal and transverse separations (such as a large $b_L$ with a small $b_T$, or a small $b_L$ with a large $b_T$) map to the same invariant $b^2$. Fitting in the $(P \cdot b, b^2)$ basis would therefore compress these distinct orthogonal spatial dependencies into a single coordinate. Fitting over the $(b_L, b_T)$ grid allows the model to separate the longitudinal decay profile from the transverse decay profile. 

Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_transformation} shows that a simple Gaussian model fails for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ and makes the Fourier integral over $P\cdot b$ diverge. Section~\ref{sec:discretization_effects} removes the data with $|\vect{b}|<3a$, which contain discretization artifacts. Section~\ref{global-bLbTeta-fit} explains why the fits must include the staple extent $\eta$ and reach the asymptotic limit $\eta\to\infty$. Section~\ref{sec:pysr_methodology} uses symbolic regression (\texttt{PySR}) with an asymptotic penalty as a guide to the functional form, which points to a power-law tail $(1+b^2)^{-j}$ multiplied by $e^{-f\eta}$. Section~\ref{sec:exponential_failure} tests exponential decay in $|\vect{b}|$ and in $P\cdot b$ as an alternative. Section~\ref{sec:systematic_exploration} compares Gaussian and power-law fits over $6\leq\eta\leq10$ and fits individual $\eta$ slices to find the $\eta$-dependence of the parameters $c$ and $d$, and Section~\ref{sec:candidate_selection} selects the model for $c(\eta)$ and gives the models for the three amplitudes.

Section~\ref{sec:simultaneous_fit_strategy} fits the three amplitudes simultaneously with the full Jackknife covariance matrix, cancels the shared $e^{-f\eta}$ factor, and evaluates the $x$-dependent Sivers shift at each of the four momenta. Section~\ref{sec:rejection_exponential_longitudinal} applies separable longitudinal and transverse models to all three amplitudes.


\subsection{Failure of Simple Gaussian Model}
\label{sec:gaussian_transformation}

For the real parts of the amplitudes ($\tAmp_{iB}^{\text{Re}}$), Hermitian conjugation requires the function to be even in the longitudinal separation $b_L$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq-ReIm-parity}). As a starting point, we model the real amplitude with a standard 2D Gaussian centered at the origin
\begin{align}
    \tAmp_{iB}^{\text{Re}}&=\gamma \exp{\left[-\alpha b_L^2-\beta b_T^2\right]}
\end{align}
To transform this into the invariant variable space, we substitute $b_T^2 = b^2 - b_L^2$ and apply the Lorentz-invariant projection $b_L = (P \cdot b) / (-P_L)$ derived in Section \ref{sec:lorentz_mapping}. Squaring this substitution gives
\begin{align}
   \tAmp_{iB}^{\text{Re}} &=\gamma \exp{\left[-\frac{\left(\alpha-\beta\right)}{P_{L}^2} (P\cdot b)^{2}-\beta b^{2}\right]}
\end{align}

For the Fourier integral $\int d(P\cdot b) e^{ix(P\cdot b)} \tAmp_{iB}$ to converge, the argument of the exponential must remain negative as $(P \cdot b) \to \pm \infty$. This requires $(\alpha - \beta) > 0$. If $\alpha < \beta$, the amplitude grows exponentially at large longitudinal separations and the integral diverges.

However, fitting this simple Gaussian model to our lattice data yields $\alpha < \beta$ for the unpolarized real amplitude, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, as shown for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$ in Figures ~\ref{fig:gaussian_failure_A2B}-\ref{fig:gaussian_failure_A12B}.

\subsection{Discretization Effects and Short-Distance Data Exclusion}
\label{sec:discretization_effects}

The Gaussian fit of Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_transformation} includes quark separations $\vect{b}$ at short distance ($|\vect{b}| \sim 1a$ or $2a$) as well as in the medium range ($|\vect{b}|\ge 3a$). The short-distance regime is governed by different physics, namely perturbative effects, including incipient short-distance divergences that require additional renormalization, while we aim to probe the medium-range non-perturbative TMD physics. A fit that includes the short-distance data would have to account for this crossover between regimes even without discretization artifacts. Before exploring further models, we therefore account for the limitations of discretized spacetime and keep only the data that probe non-perturbative TMD physics.

As discussed in Chapter \ref{sec:lattice_methodology}, Lattice QCD defines the theory on a discrete grid with a finite lattice spacing $a$. Observables evaluated at length scales comparable to $a$ contain discretization artifacts. At short quark-separation distances ($|\vect{b}| \sim 1a$ or $2a$), the continuous $O(4)$ rotational symmetry of Euclidean QCD breaks down to the discrete hypercubic group of the lattice. The step-wise Wilson lines connecting these closely spaced fields also introduce $\mathcal{O}(a)$ and $\mathcal{O}(a^2)$ finite-spacing errors \cite{Gattringer:2010zz, Lin:2014}. Fitting continuum models to these short-distance points would parameterize ultraviolet lattice artifacts rather than the nucleon's non-perturbative structure.

To separate our parameterizations from these short-distance effects, we apply a spatial cut to our dataset before fitting. We exclude data points where the magnitude of the quark spatial separation is less than three lattice units:
\begin{equation}
    |\vect{b}| = \sqrt{b_L^2 + b_T^2} \ge 3a \ .
\end{equation}

With the dataset restricted to $|\vect{b}| \ge 3a$, the non-local operator spans enough lattice sites for the step-wise gauge link to approach a continuous trajectory. The fits and subsequent Fourier transforms then depend on long-range dynamics rather than the ultraviolet regulator. 

The lattice data themselves show the difference between the two regions (Figure~\ref{short-dis1}). At fixed $b^2$, the short-distance unpolarized amplitude rises with $|P\cdot b|$ by about $14\%$ for $b^2/a^2=5$, while the long-distance amplitude stays flat within uncertainties for $b^2/a^2=20$ and $45$.

    \label{short-dis1}
\end{figure}


The fits we do are global fits over the full $(b_L, b_T, \eta)$ grid, equivalently over $(P\cdot b, b^2, \eta)$. They do not model the amplitude at any single value of $b^2$, so what matters is the behavior of the data set as a whole rather than the trend of each individual $b^2$ group. Isolated groups at $|\vect{b}| \ge 3a$ can follow the short-distance trend within their uncertainties, as $b^2/a^2 = 20$ does at $\eta|\vect{v}|/a = 6$, $7$ and $8$. However, the fit is constrained by all data at $|\vect{b}| \ge 3a$ together. Therefore, all later stability analyses, global fits, and extracted phenomenological parameters in this chapter use only the $|\vect{b}| \ge 3a$ dataset. 


\subsection{Global fit in \texorpdfstring{$(b_L,b_T,\eta)$}{TEXT} (equivalently in \texorpdfstring{$(P\cdot b,b^2,\eta)$}{TEXT})}
\label{global-bLbTeta-fit}
As discussed in Section \ref{sec:link_geometry}, we require the staple extent $\eta$ to reach infinity, $\eta|v|\rightarrow\pm\infty$, as a crucial condition, thereby representing the generalized Sivers shifts (Eq.~\eqref{eq-genSivShift}) for SIDIS and DY. The first Mellin-moment ($x$-integrated) calculation of generalized Sivers shifts \cite{Engelhardt:2015xja} shows that the Sivers shift ratio reaches an asymptotic plateau in the region $|\eta| |v|=7a \ldots 12a$. This means the Sivers TMD and the unpolarized TMD share a common $\eta$ profile that cancels in the asymptotic limit, giving the Sivers shift ratio a plateau at large $\eta$.

In the current work, we extract the $x$ dependence (we will not integrate over $x$). Since $(P\cdot b)$ (the Fourier conjugate of $x$) and the staple extent $\eta$ are independent for fixed $\hat{\zeta}$ (Eq.\eqref{eq-zeta-again}), we expect the $x$-dependent Sivers TMD and the unpolarized TMD to share a common $\eta$ profile that cancels in the asymptotic limit. This expected $\eta$ profile does not necessarily factorize into $\eta$ and $(b_L,b_T)$ for lattice amplitudes, since cancellation occurs only in the asymptotic limit. We will explore a Global fit in $(P_L,b_T,\eta)$ (equivalently in $(P\cdot b,b^2,\eta)$) for amplitudes which includes factorized $\eta$ profiles and also coupled to $(P_L,b_T)$ anzertz. Also, note that we are interested in the asymptotic limit; we include the relatively larger (approaching SIDIS and DY limits) $\eta$ range $6\leq\eta\leq10$ for all later stability analyses, global fits, and extracted phenomenological parameters from lattice amplitudes.  


\subsection{Physics-Informed Symbolic Regression via PySR}
\label{sec:pysr_methodology}

We utilized machine learning (ML) methods, specifically Symbolic Regression (SR), to explore multidimensional functional forms for lattice amplitudes as an aid.

Unlike traditional regression, which optimizes the parameters of a pre-determined function, symbolic regression searches the space of mathematical expressions and their parameters simultaneously. We use \texttt{PySR} \cite{Cranmer2023pysr}, a framework that uses Genetic Programming to discover interpretable equations from data by balancing accuracy and simplicity.

Section~\ref{sec:pysr_config} describes the search configuration, Section~\ref{sec:pysr_loss} the custom loss function, and Section~\ref{sec:pysr_pareto} the resulting Pareto front.

\subsubsection{Expression Search and Configuration}
\label{sec:pysr_config}
\texttt{PySR} \cite{Cranmer2023pysr, Koza1992GP, Schmidt2009Distilling} uses a genetic algorithm to iteratively evolve mathematical expressions represented as binary trees. The algorithm evaluates a population of expressions against the lattice data using a custom loss function over thousands of generations. 

Table~\ref{tab:pysr_config} lists the \texttt{PySR} search configuration: the permitted mathematical operators, the nesting constraints, and the main hyperparameters. The operator library includes a custom Lorentzian-type binary operator, $\text{lor}(x, y) = 1/(1 + x^2 + y^2)^2$, alongside standard arithmetic and exponential functions. Nesting constraints prevent the algorithm from generating physically meaningless double-exponential or double-decay structures.

\subsubsection{The Physics-Informed Custom Loss Function}
\label{sec:pysr_loss}

Standard symbolic regression optimizes metrics like Mean Squared Error (MSE). However, a genetic algorithm might discover a polynomial or diverging exponential function that fits the finite $(b_L, b_T)$ lattice grid well, but diverges as $b_L \to \infty$. As shown in Section \ref{sec:gaussian_transformation}, this would cause the subsequent Fourier transform over $P \cdot b$ to diverge.

We implemented a custom loss function within \texttt{PySR} to enforce the required asymptotic decay. The reduced chi-squared statistic determines the base accuracy of the model:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{base}} = \frac{\chi^2}{\text{dof}} = \frac{1}{N - k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \frac{\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re, lattice}}(i) - \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re, model}}(i)}{\sigma_i} \right)^2
\end{align}

Here, $N$ is the number of lattice data points, $k$ is the number of degrees of freedom in the generated expression, and $\sigma_i$ is the statistical error of the $i$-th lattice point.

To enforce the asymptotic decay required for Fourier integrability, we introduce a penalty term. We evaluate the proposed model equation at large values of the longitudinal separation, $b_L^{\text{large}}$, while keeping the transverse separations $b_T$ fixed to physical slices. We compute the sum of the absolute model predictions in this asymptotic regime:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{P}_{\text{asymptotic}} = \sum_{b_T \in \{b_{T}^{\text{fixed}}\}} \sum_{b_L \in \{b_{L}^{\text{large}}\}} \left| \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re, model}}(b_L, b_T) \right|
\end{align}

If a generated equation diverges or grows at large $b_L$, the penalty term $\mathcal{P}_{\text{asymptotic}}$ increases, lowering the equation's fitness score. If the equation decays toward zero, the penalty vanishes.

We combine these terms into the total objective function for \texttt{PySR}, with the penalty scaled by a small regularization parameter $\epsilon$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}} &= \mathcal{L}_{\text{base}} + \epsilon \, \mathcal{P}_{\text{asymptotic}} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}} &= \frac{\chi^2}{\text{dof}} + \epsilon \sum_{b_T \in \{b_{T}^{\text{fixed}}\}} \sum_{b_L \in \{b_{L}^{\text{large}}\}} \left| \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re, model}}(b_L, b_T) \right|
\end{align}

By evaluating fitness with $\mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}}$, \texttt{PySR} restricts the search to mathematical expressions that fit the finite-lattice data and satisfy the asymptotic decay constraints. The Julia implementation of this objective function is shown below. The asymptotic penalty evaluates each candidate expression at five test coordinates in the regime $b_L \gg 1$, spanning staple extents $\eta \in \{6, 8, 10\}$ and longitudinal separations up to $b_L = 80a$. We set the regularization weight to $\epsilon = 0.1$.

\subsubsection{Pareto Front of Discovered Models}
\label{sec:pysr_pareto}

Symbolic regression outputs a Pareto front of candidate models: a ranked list of equations that trade off mathematical simplicity (measured by expression tree complexity $C$) and predictive accuracy (measured by the custom loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}}$). Table~\ref{tab:pysr_pareto_front} lists the Pareto front for the real unpolarized amplitude $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$.

\input{PySR_outputs/pysr_equations_table}

At the lowest complexities ($C \leq 4$), \texttt{PySR} isolates the overall $\eta$-dependence of the amplitude as an exponential decay $\sim (0.499)^\eta \approx e^{-0.695\eta}$. At intermediate complexities ($C = 7$ to $12$), the algorithm introduces spatial $b$-dependence through Lorentzian-type power-law denominators $(1 + b_L^2 + b_T^2)^{-j}$ alongside linear or power-law $\eta$-models. The algorithm does not select a Gaussian spatial profile $e^{-cb^2}$ %nor an exponential decay in $b$ 
at any point along the Pareto front. Instead, it converges on the power-law tail structure.

The models at complexities $C = 14$ and $C = 16$ best balance accuracy and structure. The $C = 14$ expression has a factorized form:
\begin{equation}
    \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} = \frac{39.71}{(1 + b_L^2 + b_T^2)^{1.39}} \, e^{-0.534\eta} \ ,
    \label{eq:pysr_C14}
\end{equation}
which is a product of an exponential decay in $\eta$ and a generalized Lorentzian power-law decay in $b^2 = b_L^2 + b_T^2$. The $C = 16$ model introduces a small additive constant offset of $-0.00127$, reducing the loss to $\mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}} = 2.03$:
\begin{equation}
    \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} = \frac{245.5}{(1 + b_L^2 + b_T^2)^{1.26}} \, e^{-0.468\eta} - 0.00127 \ .
    \label{eq:pysr_C16}
\end{equation}
Beyond $C = 16$, added complexity yields only marginal improvements in loss while reducing interpretability.

The power-law tail $(1 + b^2)^{-j}$ decays more slowly than a Gaussian but, for $j > 1/2$, is absolutely integrable over the real line, so unlike the Gaussian model of Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_transformation} it guarantees convergent Fourier integrals. The factorized structure $e^{-f\eta} \times g(b^2)$ also allows the shared $\eta$-dependent exponential to cancel analytically in the Sivers shift ratio, which we use in Section~\ref{sec:simultaneous_fit_strategy}.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pysr_benchmark_fits}, the factorized $C = 14$ and $C = 16$ expressions track the lattice data throughout the accessible $(b_L, b_T)$ grid across all staple extents. The pure power-law-$\eta$ models ($C = 11, 17$) show slight systematic tension at the boundary $\eta$ values, where their $\eta^{-n}$ dependence departs from the exponential decay.

The straight-line diagnostic in Figure~\ref{fig:gaussian_straightline_test} confirms the preference for a power-law tail over Gaussian fall-off: the lattice data curve upward at large $b^2$ rather than following the Gaussian's expected straight line, consistent with the generalized Lorentzian power-law tail discovered by the genetic algorithm.

The \texttt{PySR} search also has a structural limitation. Every model on the front from $C = 7$ to $C = 21$ depends on $b_L$ and $b_T$ only through $b_L^2 + b_T^2 = b^2$, with no separate parameter for $b_L$. Since $b_L = (P\cdot b)/(-P_L)$, these expressions are independent of $P\cdot b$ once mapped to the Lorentz-invariant variables, and they cannot describe the $P\cdot b$ dependence that the Fourier transform to $x$ requires. \texttt{PySR} therefore served as a guide for the $b^2$ and $\eta$ dependence, not as the final model. Starting from Gaussian, exponential decay, and power-law forms, we systematically explored functions with $P\cdot b$ dependence in Section~\ref{sec:systematic_exploration} and tested separable alternatives in Section~\ref{sec:rejection_exponential_longitudinal}.

\subsection{Failure of Exponential Decay Model}
\label{sec:exponential_failure}
An exponential fall-off is a physically motivated alternative to the power law, but the Pareto front in Table~\ref{tab:pysr_pareto_front} contains no exponential decay in $|\vect{b}|$. The search used $(\eta, b_L, b_T)$ as inputs and had no square-root operator, so $e^{-c|\vect{b}|}$ has to be built from several nodes ($C \approx 19$ including the $\eta$ factor), while the $\text{lor}$ operator of Table~\ref{tab:pysr_config} gives a power law in $b^2$ at much lower complexity. Fitting $e^{-c|\vect{b}|}e^{-f\eta}$ directly to the same data gives a loss of about $2.63$, and adding a constant offset, as in the $C = 16$ model, lowers it to $2.26$. Both are above the loss of the simpler $C = 16$ model ($2.03$), so neither form would have stayed on the front. We therefore tested exponential decay directly, first in $|\vect{b}|$ and then in the longitudinal variable $P\cdot b$.

In Figure~\ref{fig:exp_b_straightline_test} the data lie close to the straight line of $e^{-c|\vect{b}|}$ over the accessible range $3\le|\vect{b}|/a\le 8.2$, so $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ alone does not favor the power law.

To test an exponential decay along the longitudinal direction, we fit separable models that multiply a longitudinal profile, $e^{-c|P\cdot b|}$ or $e^{-c(P\cdot b)^2}$, by a transverse profile, $e^{-d|\vect{b}|}$, $e^{-db^2}$ or $(1+db^2)^{-j}$, with the same factor $e^{-f\eta}$ (Table~\ref{tab:rejection_gaussian_transverse}). With the Gaussian transverse profile the fit is poor ($\chi^2/\text{dof} \approx 4.98$). The other four combinations reach $\chi^2/\text{dof} \approx 1.9$ to $2.2$, but in each of them the longitudinal coefficient $c$ is consistent with zero and its central value is negative. Without a positive $c$ the model does not decay in $P\cdot b$, and the Fourier transform to $x$ diverges.

\subsection{Candidate Selection}
\label{sec:candidate_selection}

The $\chi^2/\text{dof}$ minimizations and the sequential fits at individual $\eta$ slices determine the structural parameterizations for the three surviving invariant amplitudes ($\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$; $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Im}}$ remains excluded, as noted above).


In selecting the model for $c(\eta)$ for the $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ amplitude, goodness of fit must be paired with physical boundary conditions on the light-cone momentum fraction $x \in [-1, 1]$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quark versus Anti-quark contribution for unpolarized TMD}: In light-cone field theory, positive momentum fractions ($x > 0$) correspond to quark distributions $q(x) = f_1(x)$, while negative momentum fractions ($x < 0$) govern anti-quark distributions through $\bar{q}(x) = -f_1(-x)$ \cite{BMush_Engelhardt2012}. In the physical limit, at larger momentum, the correct candidate model should give a near-zero contribution for anti-quarks, i.e., for unpolarized TMDs' momentum fraction $x\lesssim-0.2$.
    \begin{align}
        \left[\tilde{f}^{(0)}_{1}(x\lesssim-0.2)=2\tAmp^{[x]}_{2B}(x\lesssim-0.2)\right]\longrightarrow0
    \end{align}
    where, $\tAmp^{[x]}_{2B}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} d(b\cdot P)e^{ix(b\cdot P)} \left[\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}(\cdots)+i\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}(\cdots)\right]$. This crucial condition involves both $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ and $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$. 
    \item \textbf{Mellin moment ($x$-integrated) calculations}: The previous work on lattice TMDs \cite{BMush_Engelhardt2012, Yoon:2015ocs, Yoon:2017qzo} calculated the first Mellin moment in $x$ ($x$-integrated TMD: $f^{[1]}(\vprp{\kei}^2;\ldots) \equiv \int_{-1}^1 dx\ f(x,\vprp{\kei}^2;\ldots)$) of the Sivers shift, which is accessible by evaluating amplitudes at $(b\cdot P)=0$:
    \begin{align}
        \langle \vect{k}_y \rangle_{TU}(\vprp{\bvec}^2;\zetahat,\eta v \tcdot P) 
	&\ \equiv\ \mN \frac{\tilde f_{1T}^{\perp[1](1)}(\vprp{\bvec}^2;\zetahat,\ldots,\eta v \tcdot P)}{\tilde f_1^{[1](0)}(\vprp{\bvec}^2;\zetahat,\ldots,\eta v \tcdot P)}  
	= - \mN \frac{\widetilde{A}_{12B}(-\vprp{\bvec}^2,0,0,-1/(\mN\zetahat)^2,\eta v\tcdot P) }{ \widetilde{A}_{2B}(-\vprp{\bvec}^2,0,0,-1/(\mN\zetahat)^2,\eta v\tcdot P) } \nonumber \\
	&\ \xrightarrow{\vprp{\bvec}^2 = 0} 
		\left. \frac{ \int dx \int d^2 \vprp{\kei}\, \vect{\kei}_y  \ \Phi^{[\gamma^+]}(x,\vprp{\kei},P,S;\ldots) }
		{ \int dx \int d^2 \vprp{\kei}  \phantom{\vect{\kei}_y}\ \Phi^{[\gamma^+]}(x,\vprp{\kei},P,S;\ldots) } \right|_{\displaystyle \vprp{S}=(1,0) } 
	\label{eq-genSivShift-x-moment}
    \end{align}
    The correct candidate model must reproduce the previous results. This means we calculate
    \begin{align}
        \tAmp^{[x]}_{2B}(x)=&\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} d(b\cdot P)e^{ix(b\cdot P)} \left[\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}(\cdots)+i\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}(\cdots)\right]\\
        \tAmp^{[x]}_{12B}(x)=&\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} d(b\cdot P)e^{ix(b\cdot P)} \left[\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}(\cdots)\right]
    \end{align}
    from candidate model for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$, then we calculate
    \begin{align}
        \int dx\, \tilde{f}_1^{(0)} =& 2\int dx\, (\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{2B} - \tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B})\\
        \int dx\, \tilde{f}_{1T}^{\perp(1)} =& -2\int dx\, \tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{12B}
    \end{align}
    to calculate $m_N \frac{\int dx\, \tilde{f}_{1T}^{\perp(1)}}{\int dx\, \tilde{f}_1^{(0)}}$. This condition involves all $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$ and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$ amplitudes. 
\end{itemize}

The model $c_2(\eta)$ (of Figure \ref{fig:c_eta_candidates}) for real unpolarized amplitude $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}=\left[\frac{a e^{-f \eta}}{\left[1 + \left(d + c\left(1 - \frac{k_1}{\eta} - \frac{k_2}{\eta^2}\right)\right) b_L^2 + d b_T^2\right]^j}\right]$ combined with the imaginary unpolarized amplitude model $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}=\left[\frac{a^{\prime\prime} b_L e^{-f \eta}}{\left[1 + (c^{\prime\prime} + d^{\prime\prime}) b_L^2 + d^{\prime\prime}b_T^2\right]^{j^{\prime\prime}}}\right]$ suppresses anti-quark contributions in above mentioned regime and has the lowest AIC (and $\chi^2/\mathrm{DoF}$). The row 1 and column 3 of Figures \ref{fig:ft_xdep_2d_P1}, \ref{fig:ft_xdep_2d_P2}, \ref{fig:ft_xdep_2d_P3}, \ref{fig:ft_xdep_2d_P4} plot the $\tilde{f}^{(0)}_{1}(x)=2\tAmp^{[x]}_{2B}(x)$, which shows near-zero contribution for anti-quark ($x\lesssim-0.2$) for higher momentum.

The selected model for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$ and along with the model for $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}=\left[\frac{-a^{\prime} e^{-f \eta}}{\left[1 + (c^{\prime} + d^{\prime}) b_L^2 + d^{\prime} b_T^2\right]^{j^{\prime}}}\right]$, produce Mellin moment ($x$-integrated) calculations of Sivers shift TMD $\langle \vect{k}_y \rangle_{TU}=m_N \frac{\int dx\, \tilde{f}_{1T}^{\perp(1)}}{\int dx\, \tilde{f}_1^{(0)}}$ which is consistent previously published results. Tables \ref{Numerical-Integration-P1}, \ref{Numerical-Integration-P2}, \ref{Numerical-Integration-P3}, \ref{Numerical-Integration-P4}, calculate the $\int dx\, \tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{2B}$, $\int dx\, (\tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B})$, $\int dx\, \tilde{f}_1^{(0)} = 2\int dx\, (\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{2B} - \tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B})$, $\int dx\, \tilde{f}_{1T}^{\perp(1)} = -2\int dx\, \tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{12B}$, and finally the Mellin moment of Sivers shift TMD $m_N \frac{\int dx\, \tilde{f}_{1T}^{\perp(1)}}{\int dx\, \tilde{f}_1^{(0)}}$ for integration limit of $[-\infty, \infty]$, $[-1, 1]$, $[-1, 0]$, and $[0, 1]$ which are consistent with previously published results.

In summary the final candidate analytical models in Cartesian $(b_L,b_T)$ space for the three amplitudes are:
\begin{align}
    \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}&=\left[\frac{a e^{-f \eta}}{\left[1 + \left(d + c\left(1 - \frac{k_1}{\eta} - \frac{k_2}{\eta^2}\right)\right) b_L^2 + d b_T^2\right]^j}\right]\label{eq:model_ReA2B}\\
    \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}&=\left[\frac{-a^{\prime} e^{-f \eta}}{\left[1 + (c^{\prime} + d^{\prime}) b_L^2 + d^{\prime} b_T^2\right]^{j^{\prime}}}\right]\label{eq:model_ReA12B}\\
    \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}&=\left[\frac{a^{\prime\prime} b_L e^{-f \eta}}{\left[1 + (c^{\prime\prime} + d^{\prime\prime}) b_L^2 + d^{\prime\prime} b_T^2\right]^{j^{\prime\prime}}}\right]\label{eq:model_ImA2B}
\end{align}



\subsection{Simultaneous Fitting Strategy}
\label{sec:simultaneous_fit_strategy}

The structural models in Eq. (\ref{eq:model_ReA2B})-(\ref{eq:model_ImA2B}) show that all three surviving amplitudes share an $\eta$-dependence damped by a common exponential decay factor, $e^{-f\eta}$. Because the Sivers shift is a ratio, this shared factor cancels in the $x$-dependent result. 

We evaluate the Sivers shift of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sivers_shift_xdep}. With $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Im}}$ dropped (Section~\ref{sec:numerical_amplitudes}) and only the even-parity combinations surviving the symmetric integration, it reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \langle \vect{k}_y \rangle_{TU} = -m_{N} \frac{\int d(b\cdot P) \cos{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)}\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}(\cdots)}{\int d(b\cdot P)\left[\cos{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)}\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}(\cdots)-\sin{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)}\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}(\cdots)\right]} \ .
\end{equation}
Substituting the analytical models from Eq. (\ref{eq:model_ReA2B})-(\ref{eq:model_ImA2B}), the shared $e^{-f\eta}$ term is independent of the integration variable $b\cdot P$ and cancels between the numerator and denominator integrals. Applying the mapping of Section~\ref{sec:lorentz_mapping} ($b_L=P\cdot b/(-P_L)$, $b_T^2=b^2-b_L^2$), we arrive at the final integrands in the invariant frame:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \langle \vect{k}_y \rangle_{TU} &= m_{N} \frac{\int d(b\cdot P) \cos{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)} \frac{a^{\prime}}{D_{12B}^{\text{Re}}}}{\int d(b\cdot P)\left[\cos{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)} \frac{a}{D_{2B}^{\text{Re}}} - \sin{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)}\frac{a^{\prime\prime} (P \cdot b) / (-P_L)}{D_{2B}^{\text{Im}}}\right]} \ , \\
    D_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= \left[1 + (c^{\prime}) \frac{(P \cdot b)^2}{P_L^2} + d^{\prime} b^2\right]^{j^{\prime}} \ , \\
    D_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= \left[1 + \left(c\left(1 - \frac{k_1}{\eta} - \frac{k_2}{\eta^2}\right)\right) \frac{(P \cdot b)^2}{P_L^2} + d b^2\right]^j \ , \\
    D_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= \left[1 + (c^{\prime\prime}) \frac{(P \cdot b)^2}{P_L^2} + d^{\prime\prime} b^2\right]^{j^{\prime\prime}} \ .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We enforce this shared $\eta$-dependence with a simultaneous global fit in which the parameter $f$ is the same in all three amplitude models ($\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$). This keeps the three models theoretically consistent and stabilizes the parameter extraction.

Section~\ref{sec:cov_fit} describes the correlated fit used at each momentum.

\subsubsection{Simultaneous Fit with Full Covariance Matrix}
\label{sec:cov_fit}

Because we extract the amplitudes from the same gauge field configurations and evaluate them across nearby spatial coordinates $(b_L, b_T)$, their statistical fluctuations are strongly correlated. An uncorrelated $\chi^2$ minimization weights these data points incorrectly, which underestimates the uncertainties and biases the central values.

We therefore perform a correlated $\chi^2$ minimization with the full covariance matrix, which accounts for the cross-correlations in the lattice data, both across different quark spatial separations and across the real and imaginary amplitude components. 

We estimate these correlations using Jackknife resampling. Let $y_i^{(k)}$ represent the lattice evaluation of the $i$-th data point (spanning all spatial coordinates across $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$) in the $k$-th Jackknife bin. The full Jackknife covariance matrix $C_{ij}$ is defined as \cite{Boram2011Covariance}:
\begin{equation}
    C_{ij} = \frac{N-1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left( y_i^{(k)} - \bar{y}_i \right) \left( y_j^{(k)} - \bar{y}_j \right) \ ,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the total number of Jackknife samples and $\bar{y}_i = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k y_i^{(k)}$ is the sample mean. 

The global simultaneous fit minimizes the correlated $\chi^2$ objective function with respect to the model parameters $\mathbf{p}$:
\begin{equation}
    \chi^2(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_{i,j} \left( \bar{y}_i - F_i(\mathbf{p}) \right) C_{ij}^{-1} \left( \bar{y}_j - F_j(\mathbf{p}) \right) \ ,
\end{equation}
where $F_i(\mathbf{p})$ represents the analytical models evaluated at the corresponding kinematics. 

We use the \texttt{lsqfit} \cite{Lepage:2001ym} non-linear least squares fitting package and the \texttt{gvar} library for Gaussian error propagation. We construct the full Jackknife covariance matrix that captures the cross-correlations between $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$. The \texttt{gvar} framework binds the combined lattice data array to the inverse covariance matrix.

From the continuous parametrization of the amplitudes, we evaluate the Fourier integrals over the invariant scalar $P \cdot b$, 
\begin{align}
    \tAmp_{2B}^{[x]\text{Re}}(x, b^2;\ldots)&=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{d(\bvec \tcdot P)}{2\pi}e^{ix(b\cdot P)}\, \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}((\bvec \tcdot P), b^2;\ldots)/\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}({\bvec}^2;\ldots)\\
    i\tAmp_{2B}^{[x]\text{Im}}(x, b^2;\ldots)&=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{d(\bvec \tcdot P)}{2\pi}e^{ix(b\cdot P)}\, \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}((\bvec \tcdot P), b^2;\ldots)/\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}({\bvec}^2;\ldots)\\
    \tAmp_{12B}^{[x]\text{Re}}(x, b^2;\ldots)&=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{d(\bvec \tcdot P)}{2\pi}e^{ix(b\cdot P)}\, \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}((\bvec \tcdot P), b^2;\ldots)/\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}({\bvec}^2;\ldots)
\end{align}
Note that, since $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Im}}$ is odd in $(b\cdot P)$, its Fourier transform gives an imaginary solution (as discussed in Eq. \eqref{reminusimag-amp}). We can calculate the Fourier-transformed TMDs using Eqs. \eqref{eq-xintderfttmds1}-\eqref{eq-xintderfttmds2}:
\begin{align}
    \tilde{f}_1^{(0)} &=2\left[\tAmp_{2B}^{[x]\text{Re}}+i(i\tAmp_{2B}^{[x]\text{Im}})\right]=2\left[\tAmp_{2B}^{[x]\text{Re}}-\tAmp_{2B}^{[x]\text{Im}}\right],\\
    \tilde{f}_{1T}^{\perp(1)} &= -2\tAmp_{12B}^{[x]\text{Re}}.
\end{align}

As we detailed in Section \ref{sec:candidate_selection}, Tables \ref{Numerical-Integration-P1}, calculate the $\int dx\, \tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{2B}$, $\int dx\, (\tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B})$, $\int dx\, \tilde{f}_1^{(0)} = 2\int dx\, (\tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{2B} - \tAmp^{[x]\text{Im}}_{2B})$, $\int dx\, \tilde{f}_{1T}^{\perp(1)} = -2\int dx\, \tAmp^{[x]\text{Re}}_{12B}$, and finally the Mellin moment of Sivers shift TMD $m_N \frac{\int dx\, \tilde{f}_{1T}^{\perp(1)}}{\int dx\, \tilde{f}_1^{(0)}}$ for integration limit of $[-\infty, \infty]$, $[-1, 1]$, $[-1, 0]$, and $[0, 1]$; the results are consistent with previous studies of $x$-moment lattice calculations \cite{BMush_Engelhardt2012, Yoon:2017qzo}.

\subsection{Separable Longitudinal and Transverse Models}
\label{sec:rejection_exponential_longitudinal}
We now apply separable longitudinal and transverse models to all three amplitudes and compare them with the selected power-law fit. Section~\ref{sec:exponential_failure} tested such profiles on $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ alone; here we use the four lowest-$\chi^2$ combinations.

The four ans\"atze combine either an exponential ($e^{-c|P\cdot b|}$) or Gaussian ($e^{-c(P\cdot b)^2}$) longitudinal profile with either an exponential ($e^{-d|b|}$) or power-law ($(1+db^2)^{-j}$) transverse envelope:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ExpPb\_Expb}}:
    \begin{align}
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= a_{\text{re}} \, e^{-c_{\text{re}}|P\cdot b| - d_{\text{re}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= a_{\text{im}} \, (P\cdot b) \, e^{-c_{\text{im}}|P\cdot b| - d_{\text{im}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= -a_{\text{reA12B}} \, e^{-c_{\text{reA12B}}|P\cdot b| - d_{\text{reA12B}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ .
    \end{align}
    
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ExpPb\_PowerLawb}}:
    \begin{align}
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= \frac{a_{\text{re}} \, e^{-c_{\text{re}}|P\cdot b| - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{re}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{re}}}} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= \frac{a_{\text{im}} \, (P\cdot b) \, e^{-c_{\text{im}}|P\cdot b| - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{im}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{im}}}} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= \frac{-a_{\text{reA12B}} \, e^{-c_{\text{reA12B}}|P\cdot b| - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{reA12B}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{reA12B}}}} \ .
    \end{align}

    \item \textbf{\texttt{GaussianPb\_Expb}}:
    \begin{align}
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= a_{\text{re}} \, e^{-c_{\text{re}}(P\cdot b)^2 - d_{\text{re}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= a_{\text{im}} \, (P\cdot b) \, e^{-c_{\text{im}}(P\cdot b)^2 - d_{\text{im}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= -a_{\text{reA12B}} \, e^{-c_{\text{reA12B}}(P\cdot b)^2 - d_{\text{reA12B}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ .
    \end{align}

    \item \textbf{\texttt{GaussianPb\_PowerLawb}}:
    \begin{align}
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= \frac{a_{\text{re}} \, e^{-c_{\text{re}}(P\cdot b)^2 - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{re}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{re}}}} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= \frac{a_{\text{im}} \, (P\cdot b) \, e^{-c_{\text{im}}(P\cdot b)^2 - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{im}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{im}}}} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= \frac{-a_{\text{reA12B}} \, e^{-c_{\text{reA12B}}(P\cdot b)^2 - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{reA12B}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{reA12B}}}} \ .
    \end{align}
\end{enumerate}

Each model has a closed-form Fourier transform, so the resulting $x$-space distributions are easy to inspect. We performed global simultaneous Jackknife fits on these four ansatze using the same kinematic cuts ($|\vect{b}| \ge 3a$, $\eta \in [6, 10]$) and shared area-law factor $e^{-f\eta}$. The numerical results show several ways in which the separable models fail. In \texttt{ExpPb\_PowerLawb}, the real unpolarized amplitude fits with $\chi^2/\text{dof} \approx 1.286$. Because $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ dominates the statistical weight, the fit quality alone can make the model look adequate. The same parameterization fails for the imaginary amplitude $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, however: the longitudinal decay width $c_{\text{im}} = 0.0045 \pm 0.0248$ is zero within errors. The model then loses its longitudinal decay profile and gives an essentially flat distribution in $P\cdot b$. The overall amplitude also flips to an unphysical negative sign ($a_{\text{im}} = -0.167$).

For the real Sivers amplitude $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$, the longitudinal parameter $c_{\text{reA12B}}$ is similarly suppressed across all separable models ($0.028 \pm 0.024$ for \texttt{ExpPb\_PowerLawb} and $0.0016 \pm 0.0061$ for \texttt{GaussianPb\_PowerLawb}). The separable models cannot capture the spin-dependent correlator's distinct longitudinal curvature without forcing the decay parameter to its lower boundary. Separating out an exponential $|P\cdot b|$ dependence introduces a discontinuous first derivative (a cusp) at $P\cdot b = 0$. For an odd-parity amplitude like $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, which must vanish linearly through the origin ($\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} \propto b_L$), the non-analytic factor $e^{-c|P\cdot b|}$ conflicts with the smooth behavior of the Euclidean correlation function. The fit responds by driving $c_{\text{im}} \to 0$ to minimize the cusp penalty.

In the Lorentz-invariant variable space $(P\cdot b, b^2)$, the longitudinal separation $b_L = (P\cdot b)/(-P_L)$ and transverse separation $b_T = \sqrt{b^2 - b_L^2}$ are geometrically coupled. Separable models artificially decouple $P\cdot b$ and $b^2$. In contrast, coupling $(P\cdot b)^2$ and $b^2$ within a single denominator,
    \begin{equation}
        D = \left[ 1 + c(\eta) \frac{(P\cdot b)^2}{P_L^2} + d\,b^2 \right]^j \ ,
    \end{equation}
preserves smooth analyticity at $P\cdot b = 0$, satisfies power-law integrability, and yields stable, positive parameters across all three amplitudes, matching the values already obtained from the global fit above (e.g., $c_{\text{im}} = 0.2253(69)$, $c_{\text{reA12B}} = 0.0773(46)$, $a_{\text{im}} = +0.267(13)$). This confirms that the coupled power-law structure adopted for the global fit is preferred over the four separable forms tested above.
