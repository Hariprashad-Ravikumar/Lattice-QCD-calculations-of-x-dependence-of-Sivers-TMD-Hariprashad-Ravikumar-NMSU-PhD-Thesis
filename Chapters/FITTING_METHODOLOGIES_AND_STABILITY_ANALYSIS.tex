\section{FITTING METHODOLOGIES AND STABILITY ANALYSIS}
\label{sec:fitting_methodologies}

The lattice data show different levels of statistical precision among the invariant amplitudes. The real part of the unpolarized amplitude, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, has small statistical uncertainties across the simulated position space, whereas the imaginary unpolarized amplitude $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$ and the real Sivers amplitude $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$ have larger gauge noise. We already discarded the imaginary Sivers amplitude $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Im}}$ because it is consistent with zero. We first establish a stable global fit for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ as the baseline for the remaining numerical analysis, as it corresponds to the leading-twist unpolarized TMD and has the highest data quality.

We parameterize the amplitudes in the Cartesian space $(b_L, b_T)$ rather than the Lorentz-invariant variable space $(P \cdot b, b^2)$. Fitting in $(b_L, b_T)$ provides a larger, more uniformly distributed set of discrete data points and resolves kinematic degeneracies. Because the squared separation distance $b^2 = b_L^2 + b_T^2$ is non-negative, multiple distinct combinations of longitudinal and transverse separations (such as a large $b_L$ with a small $b_T$, or a small $b_L$ with a large $b_T$) map to the same invariant $b^2$. Fitting in the $(P \cdot b, b^2)$ basis would therefore compress these distinct orthogonal spatial dependencies into a single coordinate. Fitting over the $(b_L, b_T)$ grid allows the model to separate the longitudinal decay profile from the transverse decay profile. 

Section~\ref{sec:discretization_effects} removes the data with $|\vect{b}|<3a$, which contain discretization artifacts. Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_transformation} shows that a Gaussian model gives $\alpha<\beta$ for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, which makes the Fourier integral over $P\cdot b$ diverge. Section~\ref{sec:pysr_methodology} searches for a functional form by symbolic regression with an asymptotic penalty and finds a power-law tail $(1+b^2)^{-j}$ multiplied by $e^{-f\eta}$. Section~\ref{sec:systematic_exploration} compares Gaussian and power-law fits over $6\leq\eta\leq10$ and fits individual $\eta$ slices to determine the $\eta$-dependence of $c$ and $d$, and Section~\ref{sec:candidate_selection} selects the model for $c(\eta)$ and gives the models for the three amplitudes. Section~\ref{sec:simultaneous_fit_strategy} fits the three amplitudes simultaneously with the full Jackknife covariance matrix, cancels the shared $e^{-f\eta}$ factor, and evaluates the $x$-dependent Sivers shift at each of the four momenta. Section~\ref{sec:factorization_test} tests whether the amplitudes factorize into separate $(P\cdot b)$ and $b^2$ profiles, first with ratios of the lattice data and then with explicit factorized fits.

\subsection{Discretization Effects and Short-Distance Data Exclusion}
\label{sec:discretization_effects}

Before fitting the lattice amplitudes, we account for the limits of the discretized spacetime. Lattice QCD defines the theory on a discrete grid with a finite lattice spacing $a$. Observables evaluated at length scales comparable to $a$ contain discretization artifacts. At short quark-separation distances ($|\vect{b}| \sim 1a$ or $2a$), the continuous $O(4)$ rotational symmetry of Euclidean QCD breaks down to the discrete hypercubic group of the lattice. The step-wise Wilson lines connecting these closely spaced fields also introduce $\mathcal{O}(a)$ and $\mathcal{O}(a^2)$ finite-spacing errors. Fitting continuum models to these short-distance points would cause the algorithm to parameterize ultraviolet lattice artifacts instead of the non-perturbative structure of the nucleon.

To separate our parameterizations from these short-distance effects, we apply a spatial cut to our dataset before fitting. We exclude data points where the magnitude of the quark spatial separation is less than three lattice units:
\begin{equation}
    |\vect{b}| = \sqrt{b_L^2 + b_T^2} \ge 3a \ .
\end{equation}

With the dataset restricted to $b \ge 3a$, the non-local operator spans enough lattice sites for the step-wise gauge link to approach a continuous trajectory. The fits and the subsequent Fourier transforms then depend on the long-range dynamics rather than on the ultraviolet regulator.

\subsection{Failure of Simple Gaussian Model}
\label{sec:gaussian_transformation}

For the real parts of the amplitudes ($\tAmp^{Re}_{iB}$), Hermitian conjugation requires the function to be even in the longitudinal separation $b_L$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq-ReIm-parity}). We model the real amplitude with a standard 2D Gaussian centered at the origin
\begin{align}
    \tAmp^{Re}_{iB}&=\gamma \exp{\left[-\alpha b_L^2-\beta b_T^2\right]}
\end{align}
To transform this into the invariant variable space, we substitute $b_T^2 = b^2 - b_L^2$ and apply the Lorentz-invariant projection $b_L = (P \cdot b) / (-P_L)$ derived in Section \ref{sec:lorentz_mapping}. Squaring this substitution gives
\begin{align}
   \tAmp^{Re}_{iB} &=\gamma \exp{\left[-\frac{\left(\alpha-\beta\right)}{P_{L}^2} (P\cdot b)^{2}-\beta b^{2}\right]}
\end{align}

For the Fourier integral $\int d(P\cdot b) e^{ix(P\cdot b)} \tAmp_{iB}$ to converge, the argument of the exponential must remain negative as $(P \cdot b) \to \pm \infty$. This requires $(\alpha - \beta) > 0$. If $\alpha < \beta$, the amplitude grows exponentially at large longitudinal separations and the integral diverges.

Fitting this Gaussian model to our lattice data yields $\alpha < \beta$ for the unpolarized real amplitude, $\tAmp^{Re}_{2B}$.

This means the amplitude decays faster in the transverse direction than in the longitudinal direction, so the expression in the $(P \cdot b, b^2)$ space is not integrable. Fig.~\ref{fig:gaussian_failure} also shows that the fit quality is poor. A symmetric 2D Gaussian decay profile is too rigid to describe the non-local operator.

Because of this divergence and poor fit quality, we cannot use the simple Gaussian model. We instead use multi-parameter functions that accommodate distinct decay rates and guarantee convergence in the Fourier domain.



\subsection{Physics-Informed Symbolic Regression via PySR}
\label{sec:pysr_methodology}

Choosing a multi-dimensional functional form by hand introduces human bias, so we use Symbolic Regression (SR) instead.

Unlike traditional regression, which optimizes the parameters of a pre-determined function, symbolic regression searches the space of mathematical expressions and their parameters simultaneously. We use \texttt{PySR} \cite{Cranmer2023pysr}, a framework that uses Genetic Programming to discover interpretable equations from data by balancing accuracy and simplicity.

Section~\ref{sec:pysr_config} describes the search configuration, Section~\ref{sec:pysr_loss} the custom loss function, and Section~\ref{sec:pysr_pareto} the resulting Pareto front.

\subsubsection{Expression Search and Configuration}
\label{sec:pysr_config}
\texttt{PySR} \cite{Cranmer2023pysr, Koza1992GP, Schmidt2009Distilling} uses a genetic algorithm to iteratively evolve mathematical expressions represented as binary trees. The algorithm evaluates a population of expressions against the lattice data using a custom loss function over thousands of generations. 

Table~\ref{tab:pysr_config} lists the \texttt{PySR} search configuration: the permitted mathematical operators, the nesting constraints, and the main hyperparameters. The operator library includes a custom Lorentzian-type binary operator, $\text{lor}(x, y) = 1/(1 + x^2 + y^2)^2$, alongside standard arithmetic and exponential functions. Nesting constraints prevent the algorithm from generating physically meaningless double-exponential or double-decay structures.

\subsubsection{The Physics-Informed Custom Loss Function}
\label{sec:pysr_loss}

Standard symbolic regression optimizes metrics like Mean Squared Error (MSE). However, a genetic algorithm might discover a polynomial or diverging exponential function that fits the finite $(b_L, b_T)$ lattice grid well, but diverges as $b_L \to \infty$. As shown in Section \ref{sec:gaussian_transformation}, this would cause the subsequent Fourier transform over $P \cdot b$ to diverge.

We implemented a custom loss function within \texttt{PySR} to enforce the required asymptotic decay. The reduced chi-squared statistic determines the base accuracy of the model:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{base}} = \frac{\chi^2}{\text{dof}} = \frac{1}{N - k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \frac{\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re, lattice}}(i) - \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re, model}}(i)}{\sigma_i} \right)^2
\end{align}

Here, $N$ is the number of lattice data points, $k$ is the number of degrees of freedom in the generated expression, and $\sigma_i$ is the statistical error of the $i$-th lattice point.

To enforce the asymptotic decay required for Fourier integrability, we introduce a penalty term. We evaluate the proposed model equation at large values of the longitudinal separation, $b_L^{\text{large}}$, while keeping the transverse separations $b_T$ fixed to physical slices. We compute the sum of the absolute model predictions in this asymptotic regime:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{P}_{\text{asymptotic}} = \sum_{b_T \in \{b_{T}^{\text{fixed}}\}} \sum_{b_L \in \{b_{L}^{\text{large}}\}} \left| \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re, model}}(b_L, b_T) \right|
\end{align}

If a generated equation diverges or grows at large $b_L$, the penalty term $\mathcal{P}_{\text{asymptotic}}$ increases, lowering the equation's fitness score. If the equation decays toward zero, the penalty vanishes.

We combine these terms into the total objective function for \texttt{PySR}, with the penalty scaled by a small regularization parameter $\epsilon$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}} &= \mathcal{L}_{\text{base}} + \epsilon \, \mathcal{P}_{\text{asymptotic}} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}} &= \frac{\chi^2}{\text{dof}} + \epsilon \sum_{b_T \in \{b_{T}^{\text{fixed}}\}} \sum_{b_L \in \{b_{L}^{\text{large}}\}} \left| \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re, model}}(b_L, b_T) \right|
\end{align}

By evaluating fitness using $\mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}}$, \texttt{PySR} restricts the search to mathematical expressions that fit the finite lattice data and obey the asymptotic decay constraints. The Julia implementation of this objective function is shown below. The asymptotic penalty evaluates each candidate expression at five test coordinates in the regime $b_L \gg 1$, spanning staple extents $\eta \in \{6, 8, 10\}$ and longitudinal separations up to $b_L = 80a$. We set the regularization weight to $\epsilon = 0.1$.

\subsubsection{Pareto Front of Discovered Models}
\label{sec:pysr_pareto}

Symbolic regression outputs a Pareto front of candidate models: a ranked list of equations that trade off mathematical simplicity (measured by expression tree complexity $C$) and predictive accuracy (measured by the custom loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}}$). Table~\ref{tab:pysr_pareto_front} lists the Pareto front for the real unpolarized amplitude $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$.

\input{PySR_outputs/pysr_equations_table}

At the lowest complexities ($C \leq 4$), \texttt{PySR} isolates the overall $\eta$-dependence of the amplitude as an exponential decay $\sim (0.499)^\eta \approx e^{-0.695\eta}$, consistent with the area-law suppression of the extended Wilson line. At intermediate complexities ($C = 7$ to $12$), the algorithm introduces spatial $b$-dependence through Lorentzian-type power-law denominators $(1 + b_L^2 + b_T^2)^{-j}$ alongside linear or power-law $\eta$-models. The algorithm does not select a Gaussian spatial profile $e^{-cb^2}$ at any point along the Pareto front. It instead converges on the power-law tail structure, in agreement with Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_transformation}.

The models at complexities $C = 14$ and $C = 16$ give the best balance between accuracy and structure. The $C = 14$ expression has a factorized form:
\begin{equation}
    \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} = \frac{39.71}{(1 + b_L^2 + b_T^2)^{1.39}} \, e^{-0.534\eta} \ ,
    \label{eq:pysr_C14}
\end{equation}
which is a product of an exponential decay in $\eta$ and a generalized Lorentzian power-law decay in $b^2 = b_L^2 + b_T^2$. The $C = 16$ model introduces a small additive constant offset of $-0.00127$, reducing the loss to $\mathcal{L}_{\text{custom}} = 2.03$:
\begin{equation}
    \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} = \frac{245.5}{(1 + b_L^2 + b_T^2)^{1.26}} \, e^{-0.468\eta} - 0.00127 \ .
    \label{eq:pysr_C16}
\end{equation}
Beyond $C = 16$, added complexity yields marginal loss improvements while reducing interpretability.

The power-law tail $(1 + b^2)^{-j}$ decays more slowly than a Gaussian but, for $j > 1/2$, is absolutely integrable over the real line, so unlike the Gaussian model of Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_transformation} it guarantees convergent Fourier integrals. The factorized structure $e^{-f\eta} \times g(b^2)$ also allows the shared $\eta$-dependent exponential to cancel analytically in the Sivers shift ratio, which we use in Section~\ref{sec:simultaneous_fit_strategy}.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pysr_benchmark_fits}, the factorized $C = 14$ and $C = 16$ expressions track the lattice data throughout the accessible $(b_L, b_T)$ grid across all staple extents. The pure power-law-$\eta$ models ($C = 11, 17$) show slight systematic tension at the boundary $\eta$ values, where their $\eta^{-n}$ dependence departs from the exponential decay.

The straight-line diagnostic in Fig.~\ref{fig:gaussian_straightline_test} confirms this: the lattice data curve upward at large $b^2$ rather than following the Gaussian's expected straight line, consistent with the generalized Lorentzian power-law tail discovered by the genetic algorithm.

We therefore adopt the Lorentzian power-law structure as the spatial profile for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$. The following sections extend this functional form to the remaining amplitudes for the global simultaneous fit.

\subsection{Candidate Selection}
\label{sec:candidate_selection}

The $\chi^2/\text{dof}$ minimizations and the sequential fits at individual $\eta$ slices determine the structural parameterizations for the three surviving invariant amplitudes ($\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$; $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Im}}$ remains excluded, as noted above).

For the unpolarized real amplitude $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, the transverse width parameter $d$ remains constant across all $\eta$, but the longitudinal width parameter $c(\eta)$ requires an asymptotic model. We select the model $c_2(\eta) = c\left(1 - \frac{k_1}{\eta} - \frac{k_2}{\eta^2}\right)$ of Fig.~\ref{fig:c_eta_candidates}, for the reasons below.

In selecting the model for $c(\eta)$, goodness of fit must be paired with physical boundary conditions on the light-cone momentum fraction $x \in [-1, 1]$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quark versus Anti-quark Domains}: In light-cone field theory, positive momentum fractions ($x > 0$) correspond to quark distributions $q(x) = f_1(x)$, while negative momentum fractions ($x < 0$) govern anti-quark distributions through $\bar{q}(x) = -f_1(-x)$.
    \item \textbf{Anti-quark Suppression at Large $|x|$}: In physical nucleons, sea anti-quark densities are concentrated strictly at low momentum fractions ($|x| \lesssim 0.1$). As $|x|$ increases, the anti-quark distribution falls rapidly to zero. At moderate-to-large values ($x < -0.2$ or $x < -0.3$), the anti-quark density is negligible.
    \item \textbf{Fourier Artifact Suppression}: Candidate models that approach the asymptote too slowly or fail to capture the curvature in $\eta$ produce unphysical oscillatory behavior or non-vanishing tails in the negative-$x$ region ($x < -0.2$) upon Fourier transformation. % TODO(Hari): name which of c_1..c_10 (Fig. c_eta_candidates) show this; the old text said ``Candidates 2 and 5'' but that numbering did not match the figure.
\end{itemize}

The model $c_2(\eta)$ suppresses antiquark contributions in this regime and has the lowest AIC (and $\chi^2/\mathrm{DoF}$) in Fig.~\ref{fig:powerlaw_eta_asymptotic}.

The analytical models in Cartesian $(b_L,b_T)$ space for the three amplitudes are:
\begin{align}
    \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}&=\left[\frac{a e^{-f \eta}}{\left[1 + \left(d + c\left(1 - \frac{k_1}{\eta} - \frac{k_2}{\eta^2}\right)\right) b_L^2 + d b_T^2\right]^j}\right]\label{eq:model_ReA2B}\\
    \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}&=\left[\frac{-a^{\prime} e^{-f \eta}}{\left[1 + (c^{\prime} + d^{\prime}) b_L^2 + d^{\prime} b_T^2\right]^{j^{\prime}}}\right]\label{eq:model_ReA12B}\\
    \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}&=\left[\frac{a^{\prime\prime} b_L e^{-f \eta}}{\left[1 + (c^{\prime\prime} + d^{\prime\prime}) b_L^2 + d^{\prime\prime} b_T^2\right]^{j^{\prime\prime}}}\right]\label{eq:model_ImA2B}
\end{align}



\subsection{Simultaneous Fitting Strategy}
\label{sec:simultaneous_fit_strategy}

The structural models in Eq. (\ref{eq:model_ReA2B})-(\ref{eq:model_ImA2B}) show that all three surviving amplitudes share an $\eta$-dependence damped by a common exponential decay factor, $e^{-f\eta}$. Because the Sivers shift is a ratio, this shared factor cancels in the $x$-dependent result. 

We evaluate the Sivers shift of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sivers_shift_xdep}. With $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Im}}$ dropped (Section~\ref{sec:numerical_amplitudes}) and only the even-parity combinations surviving the symmetric integration, it reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \langle \vect{k}_y \rangle_{TU} = -m_{N} \frac{\int d(b\cdot P) \cos{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)}\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}(\cdots)}{\int d(b\cdot P)\left[\cos{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)}\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}(\cdots)-\sin{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)}\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}(\cdots)\right]} \ .
\end{equation}
Substituting the analytical models from Eq. (\ref{eq:model_ReA2B})-(\ref{eq:model_ImA2B}), the shared $e^{-f\eta}$ term is independent of the integration variable $b\cdot P$ and cancels between the numerator and denominator integrals. Applying the mapping of Section~\ref{sec:lorentz_mapping} ($b_L=P\cdot b/(-P_L)$, $b_T^2=b^2-b_L^2$), we arrive at the final integrands in the invariant frame:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \langle \vect{k}_y \rangle_{TU} &= m_{N} \frac{\int d(b\cdot P) \cos{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)} \frac{a^{\prime}}{D_{12B}^{\text{Re}}}}{\int d(b\cdot P)\left[\cos{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)} \frac{a}{D_{2B}^{\text{Re}}} - \sin{\left(x(b\cdot P)\right)}\frac{a^{\prime\prime} (P \cdot b) / (-P_L)}{D_{2B}^{\text{Im}}}\right]} \ , \\
    D_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= \left[1 + (c^{\prime}) \frac{(P \cdot b)^2}{P_L^2} + d^{\prime} b^2\right]^{j^{\prime}} \ , \\
    D_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= \left[1 + \left(c\left(1 - \frac{k_1}{\eta} - \frac{k_2}{\eta^2}\right)\right) \frac{(P \cdot b)^2}{P_L^2} + d b^2\right]^j \ , \\
    D_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= \left[1 + (c^{\prime\prime}) \frac{(P \cdot b)^2}{P_L^2} + d^{\prime\prime} b^2\right]^{j^{\prime\prime}} \ .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We enforce this shared $\eta$-dependence with a simultaneous global fit in which the parameter $f$ is the same in all three amplitude models ($\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$). This keeps the three models theoretically consistent and stabilizes the parameter extraction.

Section~\ref{sec:cov_fit} describes the correlated fit used at each momentum.

\subsubsection{Simultaneous Fit with Full Covariance Matrix}
\label{sec:cov_fit}

Because we extract the amplitudes from the same gauge field configurations and evaluate them across nearby spatial coordinates $(b_L, b_T)$, their statistical fluctuations are strongly correlated. An uncorrelated $\chi^2$ minimization weights these data points incorrectly, which underestimates the uncertainties and biases the central values.

We therefore perform a correlated $\chi^2$ minimization with the full covariance matrix, which accounts for the cross-correlations in the lattice data, both across different quark spatial separations and across the real and imaginary amplitude components. 

We estimate these correlations using Jackknife resampling. Let $y_i^{(k)}$ represent the lattice evaluation of the $i$-th data point (spanning all spatial coordinates across $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$) in the $k$-th Jackknife bin. The full Jackknife covariance matrix $C_{ij}$ is defined as \cite{Boram2011Covariance}:
\begin{equation}
    C_{ij} = \frac{N-1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left( y_i^{(k)} - \bar{y}_i \right) \left( y_j^{(k)} - \bar{y}_j \right) \ ,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the total number of Jackknife samples and $\bar{y}_i = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k y_i^{(k)}$ is the sample mean. 

The global simultaneous fit minimizes the correlated $\chi^2$ objective function with respect to the model parameters $\mathbf{p}$:
\begin{equation}
    \chi^2(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_{i,j} \left( \bar{y}_i - F_i(\mathbf{p}) \right) C_{ij}^{-1} \left( \bar{y}_j - F_j(\mathbf{p}) \right) \ ,
\end{equation}
where $F_i(\mathbf{p})$ represents the analytical models evaluated at the corresponding kinematics. 

We use the \texttt{lsqfit} \cite{Lepage:2001ym} non-linear least squares fitting package and the \texttt{gvar} library for Gaussian error propagation. We construct the full Jackknife covariance matrix that captures the cross-correlations between $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$. The \texttt{gvar} framework binds the combined lattice data array to the inverse covariance matrix.

\subsection{Test for Factorization in $(P\cdot b)$ and $b^2$} \label{sec:factorization_test}

The global fit couples $(P\cdot b)^2$ and $b^2$ inside a single denominator. Here we ask whether a simpler, factorized description would do: Section~\ref{sec:ratio_test} uses ratios of the lattice data, and Section~\ref{sec:rejection_exponential_longitudinal} uses explicit factorized fits.

\subsubsection{Ratio Test on the Lattice Data}
\label{sec:ratio_test}

We first check directly on the lattice data whether an amplitude can be written as a product of a longitudinal and a transverse profile, $\tAmp(P\cdot b,b^2) = F(P\cdot b)\,G(b^2)$. If it can, any ratio taken at a common $b^2$ between two values of $P\cdot b$ is a constant, and any ratio taken at a common $P\cdot b$ between two values of $b^2$ does not depend on $P\cdot b$. We form
\begin{align}
    T(P\cdot b, b^2) &= \frac{\tAmp(P\cdot b, b^2)}{\tAmp(P\cdot b = 4, b^2)} = \frac{F(P\cdot b)}{F(4)} \ , \label{eq:factorization_T}\\
    B(P\cdot b, b^2) &= \frac{\tAmp(P\cdot b, b^2)}{\tAmp(P\cdot b, b^2 = 18)} = \frac{G(b^2)}{G(18)} \ , \label{eq:factorization_B}
\end{align}
where the second equality in each line holds only under factorization. $T$ should be flat in $b^2$ and $B$ should be flat in $P\cdot b$. Both ratios are computed on each Jackknife sample, and the error comes from the Jackknife spread of the ratio.

The limitation is in how the data are sampled. At fixed $\eta$ and $P_L$, the $(b_L, b_T)$ points computed on the lattice map onto an irregular set of $(P\cdot b, b^2)$ pairs, listed in Table~\ref{tab:Pb_bsq_pairs} for $\eta|v|/a = 8$ and $P_L = -1$. There are 34 pairs, but the rows at $\pm P\cdot b$ are symmetric copies of each other (identical for the real amplitudes and opposite in sign for $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$), so only $P\cdot b \ge 0$ carries independent information. At each $(P\cdot b, b^2)$ the two points with $\pm b_T$ are independent measurements, and we average them sample by sample. After the cut $|\vect{b}| \ge 3a$, that is $b^2 \ge 9$, only three values of $P\cdot b$ have more than one $b^2$: $P\cdot b = 0$ with $b^2 = 9, 16, 25, 36, 49$; $P\cdot b = 3$ with $b^2 = 18, 45$; and $P\cdot b = 4$ with $b^2 = 17, 20, 32$. No two of these rows share two $b^2$ values, so the ratios in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:factorization_T} and~\eqref{eq:factorization_B} cannot be built from data alone.

To get around this, we place the three rows on a common grid $b^2 = 18, 20, 25, 32$, the range that all three rows span. On the $P\cdot b = 0$ and $P\cdot b = 4$ rows we use quadratic interpolation in $b^2$: the value at a grid point comes from the parabola through the three data points on the same row that lie closest to it. The $P\cdot b = 3$ row has only two data points, $b^2 = 18$ and $45$, so there we use linear interpolation between them. There is no extrapolation. Table~\ref{tab:factorization_grid} lists which grid values are data and which are interpolated: 4 of the 12 are data, and the other 8 are interpolated. For $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, which is odd in $P\cdot b$ and vanishes at $P\cdot b = 0$, only the $P\cdot b = 3$ and $4$ rows are used.

For $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, the ratio of the $P\cdot b = 0$ row to the $P\cdot b = 4$ row stays between 0.90 and 1.12 over the grid, with Jackknife errors of 0.05 to 0.13. The $P\cdot b = 3$ row does not stay flat: its ratio rises from 0.93 at $b^2 = 18$ to 1.55 at $b^2 = 32$. In the bottom row, the $P\cdot b = 0$ and $4$ points agree at $b^2 = 20$ (0.82 and 0.83) and at $b^2 = 32$ (0.39 and 0.36) but differ at $b^2 = 25$ (0.64 and 0.52), while the $P\cdot b = 3$ point sits above both at every $b^2$ (0.94, 0.80 and 0.60). $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$ shows a milder rise, from 0.81 to 1.08. $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$ is compatible with flat ratios, but its errors are between 24\% and 59\% of the central values, and at $b^2 = 25$ the interpolated $P\cdot b = 4$ denominator has a signal-to-noise ratio below 2.

The deviations all come from the $P\cdot b = 3$ row, and that row is where the interpolation is least reliable. It has data only at $b^2 = 18$ and $45$, so its values at $b^2 = 20$, $25$ and $32$ come from a single straight line across a gap of 27 units in $b^2$. The amplitudes fall steeply and are convex in $b^2$ in this range, and a straight line between two points of a convex curve lies above it, so the interpolated $P\cdot b = 3$ values are too large and the ratios in the top row are pushed up. That one line fixes all three $P\cdot b = 3$ points in the bottom row, which is why their error bars look so small: the Jackknife error does not include the uncertainty of the interpolation. Because the other two rows are interpolated with parabolas, which follow the curvature better than a straight line, the $P\cdot b = 3$ values carry a larger interpolation bias than the values they are compared with, and the bias does not cancel in the ratios. The parabolas have their own limitation: between widely spaced points a parabola can lie above or below the true curve. This matters most for the $P\cdot b = 4$ value at $b^2 = 25$, which comes from the parabola through $b^2 = 17$, $20$ and $32$ and is the point where the $P\cdot b = 0$ and $4$ rows disagree in the bottom row. We therefore cannot tell whether the $P\cdot b = 3$ deviation, or the difference at $b^2 = 25$, is a genuine breaking of factorization or an interpolation artifact.

The ratio test is thus only a qualitative check. Where the interpolated values rest on closely spaced data, the $P\cdot b = 0$ and $4$ rows are compatible with factorization, but the sampling is too sparse to settle the question. We therefore test it below with explicit factorized fits, using the global simultaneous fit of Section~\ref{sec:simultaneous_fit_strategy} as the benchmark.


\subsubsection{Rejection of Separable Longitudinal and Transverse Factorization}
\label{sec:rejection_exponential_longitudinal}
Because the ratio test cannot settle the question, we fit explicitly factorized models and compare them with the coupled global fit. We constructed candidate models that multiply an independent longitudinal profile by various transverse $b^2$ profiles, including combinations of Gaussian, Lorentzian power-law, and exponential-decay forms, and evaluated each with full Jackknife fits.

The fit results are summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:rejection_gaussian_transverse} (see its caption for the poor $\chi^2/\text{dof}$ when exponential decay in $(P\cdot b)$ is paired with a Gaussian transverse profile). Even the better combinations give $\chi^2/\text{dof}\approx 2$, and the coefficient $c$ governing $(P\cdot b)$ comes out negative and consistent with zero within Jackknife uncertainties. Such a coefficient is unstable and makes the Fourier transform diverge.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{plots/Rejection-of-Separable-Exponential-Longitudinal-Decay.pdf}
    \caption{Fits testing the separability hypothesis. Pairing an exponential decay in $(P\cdot b)$ with a Gaussian transverse profile gives a poor fit ($\chi^2/\text{dof} \approx 4.98$). In models with a lower $\chi^2/\text{dof}$, the coefficient $c$ consistently fits to zero within uncertainties and is negative in each Jackknife set, so the data reject the separability hypothesis.}
    \label{fig:rejection_gaussian_transverse}
\end{figure}

We also tested the separability hypothesis with an $\eta$-dependent $c$, written $c(\eta)$, as the coefficient governing $(P\cdot b)$; this forces $c$ to be positive in the asymptotic limit. The asymptotic $c(\eta)$ lowers the $\chi^2/\text{dof}$ to $\approx 1.35$, with $c$ positive in the asymptotic limit (Fig.~\ref{fig:rejection_etadep_c}). These fits show that the lattice data prefer a power-law in $(P \cdot b)$ rather than an exponential decay in $|(P \cdot b)|$.

We studied four factorized ansatze with lower $\chi^2/\text{dof}$ and applied the same ansatze to all three invariant amplitudes ($\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$, $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, and $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$). These models combine either an exponential ($e^{-c|P\cdot b|}$) or Gaussian ($e^{-c(P\cdot b)^2}$) longitudinal profile with either an exponential ($e^{-d|b|}$) or power-law ($(1+db^2)^{-j}$) transverse envelope:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ExpPb\_Expb}}:
    \begin{align}
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= a_{\text{re}} \, e^{-c_{\text{re}}|P\cdot b| - d_{\text{re}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= a_{\text{im}} \, (P\cdot b) \, e^{-c_{\text{im}}|P\cdot b| - d_{\text{im}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= -a_{\text{reA12B}} \, e^{-c_{\text{reA12B}}|P\cdot b| - d_{\text{reA12B}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ .
    \end{align}
    
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ExpPb\_PowerLawb}}:
    \begin{align}
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= \frac{a_{\text{re}} \, e^{-c_{\text{re}}|P\cdot b| - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{re}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{re}}}} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= \frac{a_{\text{im}} \, (P\cdot b) \, e^{-c_{\text{im}}|P\cdot b| - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{im}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{im}}}} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= \frac{-a_{\text{reA12B}} \, e^{-c_{\text{reA12B}}|P\cdot b| - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{reA12B}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{reA12B}}}} \ .
    \end{align}

    \item \textbf{\texttt{GaussianPb\_Expb}}:
    \begin{align}
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= a_{\text{re}} \, e^{-c_{\text{re}}(P\cdot b)^2 - d_{\text{re}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= a_{\text{im}} \, (P\cdot b) \, e^{-c_{\text{im}}(P\cdot b)^2 - d_{\text{im}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= -a_{\text{reA12B}} \, e^{-c_{\text{reA12B}}(P\cdot b)^2 - d_{\text{reA12B}}|b|} \, e^{-f\eta} \ .
    \end{align}

    \item \textbf{\texttt{GaussianPb\_PowerLawb}}:
    \begin{align}
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}} &= \frac{a_{\text{re}} \, e^{-c_{\text{re}}(P\cdot b)^2 - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{re}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{re}}}} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} &= \frac{a_{\text{im}} \, (P\cdot b) \, e^{-c_{\text{im}}(P\cdot b)^2 - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{im}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{im}}}} \ , \\
        \tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}} &= \frac{-a_{\text{reA12B}} \, e^{-c_{\text{reA12B}}(P\cdot b)^2 - f\eta}}{\left(1 + d_{\text{reA12B}} b^2\right)^{j_{\text{reA12B}}}} \ .
    \end{align}
\end{enumerate}

Each model has closed-form analytical Fourier transforms, so the resulting $x$-space distributions are easy to inspect. The numerical results show several ways in which the factorized models fail. In \texttt{ExpPb\_PowerLawb}, the real unpolarized amplitude fits with $\chi^2/\text{dof} \approx 1.286$. Because $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Re}}$ dominates the statistical weight, the fit quality alone can make the model look adequate. The same parameterization fails for the imaginary amplitude $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, however: the longitudinal decay width $c_{\text{im}} = 0.0045 \pm 0.0248$ is zero within errors. The model then loses its longitudinal decay profile and gives an essentially flat distribution in $P\cdot b$. The overall amplitude also flips to an unphysical negative sign ($a_{\text{im}} = -0.167$).

For the real Sivers amplitude $\tAmp_{12B}^{\text{Re}}$, the longitudinal parameter $c_{\text{reA12B}}$ is similarly suppressed across all factorized models ($0.028 \pm 0.024$ for \texttt{ExpPb\_PowerLawb} and $0.0016 \pm 0.0061$ for \texttt{GaussianPb\_PowerLawb}). The factorized models cannot capture the distinct longitudinal curvature of the spin-dependent correlator without forcing the decay parameter to its lower boundary. Factoring out an exponential $|P\cdot b|$ dependence introduces a discontinuous first derivative (a cusp) at $P\cdot b = 0$. For an odd-parity amplitude like $\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}}$, which must vanish linearly through the origin ($\tAmp_{2B}^{\text{Im}} \propto b_L$), the non-analytic factor $e^{-c|P\cdot b|}$ conflicts with the smooth behavior of the Euclidean correlation function. The fit responds by driving $c_{\text{im}} \to 0$ to minimize the cusp penalty.

In the Lorentz-invariant variable space $(P\cdot b, b^2)$, the longitudinal separation $b_L = (P\cdot b)/(-P_L)$ and transverse separation $b_T = \sqrt{b^2 - b_L^2}$ are geometrically coupled. Factorized models force an artificial independence between $P\cdot b$ and $b^2$. In contrast, coupling $(P\cdot b)^2$ and $b^2$ within a single denominator,
    \begin{equation}
        D = \left[ 1 + c(\eta) \frac{(P\cdot b)^2}{P_L^2} + d\,b^2 \right]^j \ ,
    \end{equation}
preserves smooth analyticity at $P\cdot b = 0$, satisfies power-law integrability, and yields stable, positive parameters across all three amplitudes, matching the values already obtained from the global fit above (e.g., $c_{\text{im}} = 0.2253(69)$, $c_{\text{reA12B}} = 0.0773(46)$, $a_{\text{im}} = +0.267(13)$). This confirms that the coupled power-law structure adopted for the global fit is preferred over any explicit factorization of $(P\cdot b)$ and $b^2$.
